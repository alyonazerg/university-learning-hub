# Evolve 6 · Unit 7

Adapted from the teacher’s Unit 7 slides, including class debates.

## Essay · about 250 words

Write an essay of about 250 words: Our roots influence us, but they do not define us. To what extent do you agree? Discuss the roles of upbringing, inherited background and personal choice. Consider a contrasting perspective and reach a reasoned conclusion. You may use general or invented examples; personal family disclosure is not required. Do not copy task sentences or the reading preview.

## Individual questions · no preparation

1. How much can learning about ancestry tell us about who we are? What might a family tree or DNA test reveal? What might it fail to explain?

2. Would you want to take a DNA ancestry test? What benefits could it offer? What privacy or interpretation concerns would you consider?

3. Which has a greater influence on identity: genes or upbringing? How might the two interact? Support your view with a general or invented example.

4. Can discovering unexpected information about ancestry change someone’s sense of identity? Why might different people react differently?

5. What makes a family or community tradition meaningful? How can people keep it alive without following every detail exactly?

6. When can family celebrations bring people together, and when can they create pressure? How can different expectations be handled respectfully?

7. What gives a place special significance? How can returning to a place affect someone’s feelings about their roots? You may use an invented example.

8. Should endangered languages be actively preserved? What could be lost if a language disappears? What practical difficulties should be considered?

9. How can a cultural celebration be promoted to people who know little about it? Which methods would be effective? How could organisers avoid oversimplifying its meaning?

10. Do family ties create stronger obligations than friendships? Are there limits to those obligations? Explain and qualify your view.

11. What can families pass down besides genes and possessions? Which patterns are useful to keep, and which might people choose to change?

12. Our roots influence us, but they do not define us. To what extent do you agree? Discuss inherited background, personal choice, and the possibility of change.

## Dialogue cards · one minute preparation

Conversation Situation: DNA Testing: Benefits and Risks

Situation:
Your university is considering offering an ancestry-testing project to students. Discuss the benefits and risks and decide what safeguards would be needed.

Student A:
You think the project could help students understand their ancestry. Explain the benefits and respond to concerns.

Student B:
You are concerned about privacy, interpretation, and the use of genetic data. Ask questions and suggest safeguards before reaching agreement.

---

Conversation Situation: Keeping a Celebration Alive

Situation:
You are planning a cultural celebration for younger members of a community. Agree on a format that respects its heritage and attracts new participants.

Student A:
You want to preserve familiar rituals and customs. Explain their significance.

Student B:
You want to adapt the celebration to modern interests and include newcomers. Explain your ideas and negotiate a compromise.

---

Conversation Situation: Family Traditions and Personal Choice

Situation:
A family disagrees about whether everyone should attend an annual celebration. Discuss a compromise that maintains relationships and respects different circumstances.

Student A:
You believe shared celebrations help keep family bonds strong. Explain why attendance matters.

Student B:
You believe participation should be a personal choice. Explain your concerns and suggest other ways to stay connected.

---

Conversation Situation: Saving an Endangered Language

Situation:
A town can fund only one small cultural project this year. Decide how to support a local language that fewer young people use.

Student A:
You want to invest in classes and activities for young speakers. Explain the potential benefits.

Student B:
You want to prioritise recording stories and creating an accessible digital archive. Explain why this may be more practical and agree on a plan.

---

Conversation Situation: A Place With Significance

Situation:
You are planning a visit for people interested in your community’s history. Choose a place and decide how to explain its significance.

Student A:
You want to visit a well-known historical landmark. Explain what visitors could learn.

Student B:
You prefer a less famous place connected with everyday community life. Explain its value and help agree on a visit.

---

Conversation Situation: Family Bonds and Boundaries

Situation:
You are discussing whether relatives should make a greater effort than friends to repair a damaged relationship. Compare your views and agree on sensible limits.

Student A:
You think family ties create special responsibilities. Explain why making an effort can matter.

Student B:
You think relationships should depend on respectful behaviour rather than biology. Explain why boundaries can be necessary and look for common ground.
